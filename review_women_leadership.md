# Peer Review: "Still Missing at the Top: Women and Senior Leadership in the International Financial Institutions"
**Authors:** Eeshani Kandpal and Brian Webster  
**Format:** Blog post / policy brief (update to a 2023 CGD working paper)  
**Reviewer date:** April 2, 2026

---

## Prefatory Note on Genre

This is a blog post, not a traditional academic paper. The review frameworks below are applied with that in mind: standards for causal identification and statistical rigor are calibrated to the genre, while standards for clarity, accuracy, intellectual honesty, and policy relevance apply in full. Where the piece aspires to the register of applied empirical research—citing causal studies and drawing downstream inferences—those sections are held to a higher standard.

---

## 1. Edmans' Framework for Editorial Assessment

### Contribution

**Strengths:**  
The piece performs a genuinely valuable function: it compiles and updates a consistent time-series dataset on women's representation in senior IFI leadership through 2025, a task that requires non-trivial data collection effort and is not done systematically elsewhere. The finding that the World Bank has slid from near-parity to roughly 30 percent in two years, and that the IMF sits at 23.8 percent despite its own 2022 gender mainstreaming strategy, is substantively important and timely. The "backsliding" framing is a meaningful conceptual contribution to a field that often celebrates incremental progress.

**Concerns:**  
The contribution relative to the 2023 working paper is primarily data extension, not new analytical insight. The piece does not articulate what is genuinely new in its framing or conclusions versus what is simply updated numbers. If this is intended for an academic outlet, the contribution case would need strengthening. As a blog update, it is sufficient.

### Execution

**Strengths:**  
The data collection method is described at a level appropriate for a blog post. The decision to use December 2025 as the measurement point is consistent across institutions. The authors sensibly acknowledge the small-N volatility problem.

**Major concerns:**  
1. **Measurement inconsistency for the World Bank.** The authors acknowledge coding the World Bank "posed a bit of a challenge" and offer two different measures (12 people under "World Bank Group Leadership" vs. 12 + 35 Vice Presidents). The choice between these measures changes the denominator substantially and therefore the trend narrative. The piece pivots between them without a fully transparent accounting of which figures appear in which chart. This is the most serious methodological issue in the document. A reader cannot verify whether the "37.5% in 2023 to ~30% in 2025" decline is from the narrow measure, the broad measure, or a mix.

2. **Coding consistency over time.** The paper combines newly compiled data with data from the 2023 working paper, using "best judgement when aligning specific managerial levels." This is a significant methodological disclaimer that is buried in the data section. Historical comparisons are only meaningful if the same positions are coded consistently year over year. The piece does not confirm this.

3. **No denominator transparency.** Percentage figures are given throughout without reporting the absolute N for each institution-year. Given that some leadership teams have "fewer than ten people," a single appointment changes the share by 10+ percentage points. Readers cannot evaluate the stability of any individual data point.

4. **The AfDB is missing.** The piece states seven IFIs are covered (including AfDB), but AfDB is never discussed in the results. Either the findings are included silently in an aggregate or the institution was dropped; neither is explained.

### Exposition

**Strengths:**  
The writing is clear, direct, and well-paced for a blog audience. The "waterfall" metaphor for the gap between entry grades and senior leadership is vivid and effective. The final "three lessons" section is admirably concrete.

**Minor issues:**  
- The phrase "not an internal HR problem" is rhetorically strong but slightly overstates: representation is both an HR problem and a policy problem simultaneously.
- The sentence "And last but not the least" should be edited ("And last but not least" or simply "Finally").
- Figure references (Figures 1–8) are cited but the figures themselves are not reproduced in the document shared for review. The text is not fully self-contained without them.

## 2. Nyhan's Checklist for Peer Review

**1. Interaction terms:** Not applicable (no regression analysis).

**2. P-value interpretation:** Not applicable (descriptive statistics only). However, the piece implicitly treats trends in small-N series as informative without acknowledging when changes are within the noise band. The ADB section correctly notes volatility; this caveat should be applied more uniformly across institutions.

**3. Replication materials:** The authors describe their data collection method (public websites, organizational charts, IFI databases, direct requests) but do not provide a dataset or codebook. For a policy piece this is acceptable; for any academic version, replication data are essential. Even a simple supplementary table with institution-year-share-N would substantially improve the piece in its current form.

**4. Causal language:** This is the checklist item most relevant to this piece. The paragraph connecting IFI leadership gender to downstream project outcomes reads:

> "When the World Bank's senior women's share falls from near-parity to 30 percent...that is not an internal HR problem. It is a disconcerting sign that the quality of gender integration across hundreds of lending operations in dozens of countries is likely declining with it."

This is a causal inference drawn from a correlational cross-institution finding (that women-led projects show better gender mainstreaming). The piece is not entitled to this inference without much stronger caveats. The cited study examines within-institution variation in task team leaders, not senior leadership shares; the mechanism from VP-level gender composition to project-level gender mainstreaming is asserted, not demonstrated. The word "likely" provides only thin hedging.

**5. Causal assumptions:** When the authors draw on the Chattopadhyay-Duflo and related literature to argue that IFI leadership gender matters substantively, the assumptions required for that extrapolation (similar mechanisms, similar institutional contexts, similar governance structures) are not stated or interrogated. Village council randomization in India is a long way from VP appointments in Washington.

**6. Mediation models:** Not applicable.

**7. Post-treatment bias:** Not applicable.

**8. Statistical power:** No formal analysis; not required for a blog post. However, the piece should be more consistently explicit that with N < 10 per institution, year-to-year changes are highly sensitive to single appointments.

**9. Subgroup analyses:** Not applicable in the standard sense, though institution-by-institution breakdowns function as subgroups. No correction for multiple comparisons is needed here, but the varying sample sizes across institutions should be flagged when comparing trajectories.

## 3. Humphreys' Comprehensive Review Framework

### Part 1: Summary and Overall Assessment

This blog post updates a 2023 CGD working paper with two additional years of data on women's representation in senior leadership at seven major IFIs. The core descriptive finding—no institution at parity, progress uneven, some meaningful backsliding—is clearly stated and substantively important. The piece performs a monitoring and accountability function that is valuable in itself. What it does not do is advance a new theoretical argument, test a hypothesis, or provide the methodological apparatus that would make its historical comparisons fully credible. As a blog post, it succeeds. As a research paper, it would need significant strengthening.

What do I know after reading this that I did not know before? That as of December 2025, the IMF has the lowest share of women in senior leadership (23.8%) among the seven IFIs studied; that the World Bank has declined from near-parity to ~30% in recent years; and that three institutions (IDB, EIB, EBRD) are above 40%.

### Part 2: Major Themes

**Theme 1: Measurement validity and consistency (major)**

The piece's core credibility rests on comparability of measures over time and across institutions. Three problems undermine this:

- The World Bank coding problem (two measures, unclear which drives which figures) is acknowledged but not resolved.
- The combination of 2025 data with data from the 2023 working paper using "best judgement" is not defended or validated. Were the 2023 figures themselves reproduced and verified, or taken as given?
- No absolute Ns are reported, making it impossible to assess the stability of individual percentages.

**Recommendation:** Publish a supplementary table with institution, year, N women, N total, and share. Clarify which World Bank measure is used in each figure. Add a methodological appendix on coding rules.

**Theme 2: Causal inference from correlational evidence (major)**

The "Why gender parity matters" section deploys causal language and cites experimental/quasi-experimental literature to build an argument that IFI leadership gender affects borrowing-country outcomes. This is a reasonable narrative case but is presented with more confidence than the underlying evidence supports. The Chattopadhyay-Duflo result involves elected village councils in rural India under a reservation system—generalizing it to appointed vice presidents of multilateral banks requires justification. The task team leader finding (that women-supervised projects show better gender mainstreaming) is closer to the relevant setting but still operates at the project level, not the VP level. The leap from "women task team leaders improve project-level gender mainstreaming" to "a lower VP share means hundreds of operations are 'likely declining'" is not supported.

**Recommendation:** Either soften the causal framing ("there is evidence that... consistent with the view that...") or add explicit discussion of what additional evidence would be needed to establish the VP-to-operations pathway.

**Theme 3: Missing institution (moderate)**

The AfDB is listed as one of seven covered institutions but does not appear in the results narrative. This should be resolved: either include a paragraph on AfDB findings or explain why it was excluded.

**Theme 4: The "fragility" thesis needs more structure (moderate)**

The piece's central argument is that progress is "fragile"—dependent on individual leadership cycles rather than institutionalized norms. This is an important and plausible claim, but it is presented as a stylized fact rather than demonstrated. What would institutionalized versus non-institutionalized progress look like empirically? The piece gestures at this (parity at EBRD attributed to an explicit institutional strategy; backsliding at World Bank attributed to leadership turnover) but does not present evidence that distinguishes the two mechanisms. If this were a research paper, the hypothesis would need to be operationalized and tested.

**Theme 5: Policy implications follow logically but lack specificity (minor)**

The three recommendations at the end (pipelines insufficient, appointments matter, transparency critical) are well-grounded in the data. However, they are fairly generic. "Embedding gender criteria into selection processes" is the right direction but leaves open what criteria, who monitors them, and what the enforcement mechanism would be. Given that this is a CGD piece aimed at influencing IFI governance, more concrete recommendations (e.g., board-level disclosure requirements, gender-balanced shortlists for VP appointments, published succession planning criteria) would strengthen impact.

**Theme 6: Credibility vs. the institutions' own disclosures (minor)**

The authors note they "requested data directly from the IFIs" and incorporated what was shared. They do not indicate which institutions provided data, which did not, and whether the provided data was used to correct or supplement public information. This matters for assessing whether the figures reflect what institutions want to be known versus a complete picture.

### Part 3: Smaller Issues

- "Not a single IFI at parity" is the headline but the piece also notes IDB at 44.6% and EIB at 44.4%. A brief discussion of what "parity" means (50/50? within some band?) would sharpen the analysis.
- The EBRD at 42.5% is described as "one of the more encouraging trajectories" but also as sensitive to leadership cycles—the same tension is true of the World Bank, which previously reached near-parity. The piece could more explicitly theorize what differentiates durable from transient progress.
- Figure 8 is noted as not appearing in the original working paper, but no explanation is given for why it appears now or what it adds.
- The piece refers to "industry reports" and "databases" in its data description without citation. These should be identified.
- The prose occasionally conflates IFI senior leadership representation with managerial effectiveness in ways that move faster than the evidence: "managers and directors may influence implementation, but it is the presidents and vice presidents who shape institutional priorities." This is a strong claim about internal IFI decision-making that cites no source.
- "Last but not the least" → "last but not least."

## 4. Blattman's Empirical Paper Review Guide

### Research Question and Hypothesis

The research question—are women adequately represented in IFI senior leadership and is this improving?—is clear, important, and well-motivated. The piece is largely descriptive rather than hypothesis-testing, which is appropriate for a monitoring brief. The implicit hypothesis (that women remain underrepresented and progress is fragile) is well-supported by the data.

### Research Design

There is no causal research design, nor should there be for this type of descriptive update. The relevant design question is: is the measurement strategy consistent enough to support the trend claims? The answer is: partially. The year-over-year comparisons within institutions are plausible if coding rules have been applied consistently, but the piece provides insufficient documentation to verify this. Across-institution comparisons are fraught because institutional structures differ (some have clear VP-level roles; others have flatter or more complex hierarchies).

### Data

- Data sources are described but not fully cited.
- Small-N problem is acknowledged for ADB but should be flagged uniformly.
- The merging of 2025 data with 2023 working paper data is the key data risk. The 2023 paper presumably had its own coding rules; whether those rules were applied identically in 2025 is not confirmed.
- No missing data discussion: what happens when an IFI leadership position is vacant, acting, or interim?
- Direct data requests to IFIs: which responded? What was provided?

### Empirical Analysis

Not applicable in the causal sense. For descriptive purposes:
- Trend figures are the primary analytical tool; these seem appropriate.
- No confidence intervals or bands around the estimates; these would be informative given small denominators.
- The comparison between "narrow" and "broad" World Bank measures is potentially the most important robustness check in the piece—but it is introduced as a coding problem rather than a sensitivity analysis.

### Results

The headline results are clearly stated. The institution-by-institution narrative is informative. The lack of absolute Ns makes it impossible to assess how much weight to give any single data point. The downstream claims (IFI leadership → borrowing-country outcomes) are the weakest part of the results section and should be presented more cautiously.

### Scope

The piece covers the seven largest/most influential IFIs, which is appropriate. The authors do not discuss whether findings generalize to smaller regional development banks, UN agencies, or other multilateral bodies—an explicit scope statement would help.

## 5. Evans & Bellemare: Structure Review

### Abstract

The piece has no formal abstract, which is appropriate for a blog format. If converted to a working paper or journal article, an abstract should lead with the research question and spend most space on the specific quantitative findings (institution-level shares, magnitude of change), not the framing.

### Introduction

The opening paragraph functions as both hook and research question and does so effectively: it establishes that women are underrepresented ("strikingly"), that this has been documented before, and that this piece updates the analysis. The hook is about the economics/policy problem, not the literature—correct.

**Gap:** There is no explicit value-added paragraph. The piece states it "revisits and updates" the 2023 paper but does not articulate what is analytically new. For any academic version, a dedicated paragraph explaining what readers learn here that they could not learn from the 2023 paper is essential.

**Literature integration:** The piece appropriately weaves prior work (Chattopadhyay-Duflo, the task team leader study, cross-national legislative studies) into the argument rather than providing a standalone literature review section. However, the literature is deployed in the service of a causal argument rather than to establish the scholarly context for the descriptive contribution.

### Conclusion

The conclusion follows a reasonable structure: summary → evidence of fragility → three policy lessons. It does not include:
- **Explicit limitations section:** The piece would benefit from a paragraph acknowledging the measurement challenges, small-N volatility, and the limits of public data.
- **Future research:** What data would make this analysis more conclusive? Longitudinal tracking of individual appointments? Access to IFI internal HR data? Survey data on promotion practices?
- The policy recommendations are present but could be more specific (see Theme 5 above).

On Bellemare's formula: the conclusion summarizes ("not one institution at parity"), gestures at limitations implicitly, includes policy implications, but lacks a forward-looking research agenda.

## Summary: Priority Recommendations

### For the current blog post version

1. **Publish a supplementary table** with institution, year, N women, N total, share. This single addition would substantially raise the piece's credibility and allow readers to assess small-N volatility.

2. **Clarify the World Bank measurement.** Specify which measure (12 vs. 47) is used in each figure and in the headline "37.5% to 30%" claim.

3. **Soften the causal language** in the "Why it matters" section, or add an explicit caveat distinguishing the correlational IFI evidence from the experimental literature cited.

4. **Include AfDB results** or explain the omission.

5. **Correct "last but not the least"** and verify all figure references are resolved.

### If developed into a working paper or journal article

6. Provide a formal methodological appendix documenting coding rules, position definitions by institution, and how 2025 data were harmonized with 2023 data.

7. Add confidence intervals or ranges around the trend estimates.

8. Operationalize the "fragility" thesis more explicitly: what is the hypothesis, and what evidence tests it?

9. Explicitly state scope conditions and what the analysis does and does not generalize to.

10. Add a limitations section and future research agenda to the conclusion.

---

## Overall Assessment

This is a well-written, timely, and policy-relevant update to an important monitoring exercise. The descriptive findings are clear and the writing is effective for a blog audience. The piece's main weaknesses are: (a) insufficient documentation of measurement choices to fully support the trend claims it makes; (b) causal language in the "Why it matters" section that runs ahead of the evidence; and (c) the missing AfDB analysis. None of these would prevent publication as a blog post with minor revisions. A version intended for academic publication would require substantially more methodological transparency and a more carefully delimited causal argument.
