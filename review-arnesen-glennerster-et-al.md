# Peer Review: "Creating Markets for Carbon Removal"
**Arnesen, Glennerster, Murray-Leclair, Singh, Slaski, Snyder, and Trueswell (March 2026)**

---

## Framework 1: Edmans' Editorial Assessment

### Contribution

**Strengths.** The paper addresses a genuinely important and timely question: how to sequence public and private investment in durable carbon removal to achieve cost reductions at scale. The three-stage framework (portfolio experimentation → advance purchase commitments → compliance market integration) is a coherent and policy-relevant synthesis that goes beyond existing advocacy for any single mechanism. The counterintuitive insight that auction reserve prices should *equal* the social cost of carbon — rather than be set conservatively — to maximize entry and drive costs *down* is the paper's most intellectually distinctive claim.

**Concerns.** The brief's contribution is difficult to evaluate independently because it largely serves as a summary vehicle for the companion paper (Arnesen et al., 2026), which is unpublished. The brief cannot stand fully on its own: central claims about optimal auction design, the superiority of future auctions over early offtake agreements, and the welfare properties of the proposed mechanism are attributed to the companion paper without the reader being able to assess the underlying assumptions. This creates a circular dependency that weakens both documents.

### Execution

**Strengths.** The illustrative use of Frontier and the EU ETS is effective. The comparison between future-auction commitments and early offtake agreements (like UK offshore wind) is a useful framing of the core policy trade-off.

**Major concerns:**
- There is no original analysis in this brief. All formal results are deferred to Arnesen et al. (2026). For a brief this is acceptable, but the policy recommendations are sometimes stated with more certainty than the underlying (inaccessible) analysis can support.
- The mechanism's feasibility depends critically on *who* makes the binding commitment — governments, philanthropies, a coalition — and these actors face very different commitment problems, incentive structures, and time horizons. The brief blurs this distinction, treating "funders" as a monolithic entity.
- The political economy of compliance market integration (Stage 3) is underexplored. The EU ETS has resisted carbon removal credits for reasons that include not just additionality concerns but also political opposition from incumbent sectors and member states. The brief frames this as a technical MRV problem when it is largely a political problem.
- The social cost of carbon used as the reserve price ceiling ($250/ton) is contentious. It is derived from EPA estimates that are politically contested, jurisdiction-specific, and substantially uncertain. Whether a binding international commitment mechanism could credibly use this figure — especially across a "coalition of funders" — is not addressed.

### Exposition

**Strengths.** The brief is clearly written, well-organized, and appropriately concise at five pages. The bold-text lead sentences function effectively as a structured argument that readers can scan. The target audience (policy practitioners and funders) is served well by the accessible prose.

**Minor issues:**
- The paper lacks any figures or tables. A simple cost-trajectory diagram or a table comparing Stages 1–3 would substantially aid comprehension.
- The transition from Stage 1 (Frontier) to Stage 2 (large-scale future auction) is described in terms of a distinction between startups and large firms, but the logic connecting firm size, capital access, and information advantage is asserted rather than established.

## Framework 3: Humphreys' Comprehensive Review

### Part 1: Summary

This brief argues that durable carbon removal will require a deliberate three-stage market-creation sequence: early portfolio experimentation, credible large-scale advance purchase commitments conditional on cost reductions, and eventual integration into compliance markets. The most novel contribution is the specific design of Stage 2 — future auctions with reserve prices set at the social cost of carbon — which the authors argue dominates both targeted deals and early offtake agreements when large sums are at stake. The brief draws on a companion paper for its formal analysis and uses Frontier as a concrete institutional example of Stage 1.

What does the reader know after reading this piece that they did not know before? The reader learns that there is a theoretically grounded case for (a) the specific *sequencing* of carbon removal market creation and (b) non-conservative auction reserve prices as an entry-inducement strategy. These are genuinely useful policy insights. However, because the formal foundations are in the companion paper, the brief functions more as a well-organized advocacy document than as a self-contained analytical contribution.

**Overall assessment:** Timely, well-motivated, and clearly targeted at practitioners. The core argument is sound, but the brief overstates the certainty of its recommendations given the inaccessibility of the underlying analysis and the absence of any engagement with political economy obstacles.

---

### Part 2: Major Themes

**Theme 1: The "Financing Gap" Argument Is Asserted, Not Established**

The central premise — that market forces will not generate sufficient investment even with eventual ETS integration — rests on a knowledge-spillover and appropriability argument. This is a standard public-goods justification for subsidy and is plausible. However, the magnitude of the gap, the rate at which ETS prices will rise relative to carbon removal costs, and the degree to which compliance markets alone might be insufficient are all empirically contested. The brief treats these as settled facts. A fuller treatment would quantify the expected private vs. social returns to CDR R&D, or at minimum cite existing literature on green technology spillovers.

**Theme 2: The Information Asymmetry Mechanism Is Central but Underdeveloped**

The paper's key claim for why Stage 2 should use large firms rather than targeted startup deals rests on an information asymmetry: large firms know more than funders about whether costs will fall. This is plausible, but:
- It is not obvious that large established firms have better information about novel CDR technologies than specialized startups or early investors in those technologies.
- The paper does not explain how the auction design resolves, rather than merely exploits, this asymmetry.
- If large firms have private information about cost trajectories, they may shade their bids strategically in ways that the brief's preferred auction design does not address.

**Theme 3: The Comparison to Solar and Offshore Wind Is Underexplored**

The paper uses solar (89% cost reduction) and UK offshore wind (Contract for Difference) as positive analogies, and tidal power as a cautionary tale. But these analogies do more analytical work than they are given credit for. Solar's cost reduction was driven by Chinese manufacturing scale and specific learning-by-doing dynamics. Offshore wind's CfD mechanism worked in part because the technology was already on a clear learning curve. The brief does not explain which features of these cases generalize to CDR technologies that are currently at much earlier stages of development and have very different cost structures.

**Theme 4: Political Economy and Commitment Credibility**

The brief argues that the binding nature of the commitment is essential — firms must be confident funders will not back out. But the paper says almost nothing about how this credibility is achieved in practice. Legal contracts and pre-committed funds are mentioned, but:
- Who are the funders? Philanthropies (which face governance and succession challenges), governments (which face political-cycle problems), or corporations (which face shareholder pressure)?
- A coalition of funders introduces coordination and free-rider problems that are not addressed.
- The EU ETS has undergone multiple design revisions in response to political pressure. Why would a new procurement mechanism be more credible?

**Theme 5: MRV Standards and Additionality**

The brief correctly identifies rigorous MRV and additionality as necessary conditions for integrating CDR credits into compliance markets. However, it treats this as a solved or solvable technical problem. In fact, measurement of carbon removal is technology-specific and in many cases deeply uncertain (especially for ocean alkalinity enhancement). The additionality problem for engineered CDR, while less severe than for nature-based solutions, is not trivial — baseline energy consumption, lifecycle emissions, and permanence all require ongoing verification. The brief would benefit from briefly acknowledging the state of MRV science and what governance institutions would need to develop it.

---

### Part 3: Smaller Issues

- The social cost of carbon ($250/ton) is cited from a 2023 EPA report. This figure is jurisdiction-specific, politically contested, and subject to significant methodological dispute. A brief designed for international uptake should note this uncertainty and discuss whether a range is more appropriate.
- The brief conflates the volume of ETS revenues ($100 billion globally) with the scale of demand that would be available for CDR credits. These are not the same thing; the share of ETS demand that would be redirectable to CDR is much smaller and depends on permit price trajectories and sector coverage.
- The companion paper (Arnesen et al., 2026) is cited 11 times in 5 pages but is unpublished. This creates an unusually high dependency on inaccessible work. At minimum, the working paper should be made available alongside this brief.
- The transition between "targeted deals" (Frontier-style) and "large-scale future auction" is described as being driven by firm type (startups vs. large firms), but in practice both types of firms operate in CDR markets simultaneously. The mechanism for transitioning between stages — who decides, when, on what criteria — is not specified.
- The brief does not address the possibility that compliance-market integration could *crowd out* innovation by locking in early technologies before cost reductions are achieved (a concern raised in some innovation economics literature on patent races and lock-in).
- No mention of developing country contexts. The three-stage framework is implicitly designed for the EU and US. The political and financial feasibility in lower-income country contexts — where some CDR technologies (enhanced weathering, afforestation) have comparative advantages — is not addressed.

## Framework 4: Blattman's Empirical Paper Review

*Note: This is a policy brief, not an empirical paper. The framework is applied to the policy argument rather than an empirical identification strategy.*

**Research Question:** Clearly defined and important. The question — how to design markets that bring down CDR costs on the timeline required for net-zero — is well-motivated and specific.

**Research Design / Policy Mechanism:** The three-stage framework is intuitive and well-structured. The auction design recommendation (future auctions > early offtake when large sums are at stake) is the most specific and testable claim, but the conditions under which it holds are in the companion paper.

**Theory/Model:** The theoretical backbone is sound in principle — spillovers justify subsidy; information asymmetry justifies mechanism design; commitment credibility requires legal/financial pre-commitment. But the specific results (e.g., reserve price = social cost of carbon is optimal) depend on a formal model whose assumptions are not stated here. The reader cannot assess whether the results are fragile.

**Data:** Illustrative data points are appropriately chosen but not systematically analyzed. The comparison between solar, tidal, and CDR technologies would benefit from a table of learning rates, capital structures, and government support levels.

**Scope:** The brief is implicitly scoped to OECD contexts. CDR technologies with comparative advantages in the Global South are not discussed. The brief could note whether and how the framework might extend to multilateral or development finance contexts.

## Framework 5: Evans & Bellemare — Structure Assessment

### Abstract / Opening

The opening two paragraphs function well as a hook: they establish the scale of the CDR problem (IPCC targets, current cost of DAC) and the market failure (front-loaded financing, knowledge spillovers) that motivates the policy proposal. The research question is stated in paragraph three. This is effective for a policy brief.

**Minor issue:** The opening sentence ("Reducing current emissions will not solve the climate crisis alone...") is strong but the second paragraph begins with "Large-scale carbon removal requires a strategy..." — this is a transition to the proposed solution, not a deepening of the problem. A brief additional sentence on *why* markets currently fail to provide this strategy would strengthen the hook-to-solution bridge.

### Introduction Structure (Head/Evans Formula)

| Element | Present? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook (why Y matters) | Yes | IPCC targets, DAC costs, ETS revenues |
| Research question | Yes | Three-stage framework stated early |
| Antecedents | Partial | Frontier is discussed; broader CDR market literature is not |
| Value-added | Weak | Not clearly distinguished from existing AMC/advance commitment literature |
| Roadmap | Absent | The brief would benefit from a one-sentence preview of the argument's structure |

### Conclusion Structure (Bellemare Formula)

| Element | Present? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Summary (tell them what you told them) | Yes | Concise recap of three stages |
| Limitations | Absent | No discussion of when the framework might fail |
| Policy implications | Partial | Present but cursory; no cost-benefit framing |
| Future research | Absent | No mention of open questions |

**Major gap:** The conclusion is very short (one paragraph) and reads as a repetition of the introduction's thesis. It does not acknowledge: (1) the conditions under which the three-stage approach might fail; (2) the political economy obstacles to Stage 2 credibility; (3) what empirical or experimental evidence would help validate the auction design recommendation; or (4) the international governance architecture needed for a coalition commitment of this type.

## Summary of Key Recommendations

**Major concerns (potential grounds for substantive revision):**
1. The companion paper (Arnesen et al., 2026) is unpublished and carries most of the analytical weight. Either the key assumptions and results should be stated in this brief, or the companion paper should be released simultaneously.
2. The political economy of commitment credibility — who the funders are, how they coordinate, how they resist renegotiation — is the central practical challenge and is not addressed.
3. The conclusion needs to be substantially expanded to address limitations, implementation conditions, and open research questions.

**Minor issues:**
1. Add a figure or table comparing the three stages (mechanism, funder type, time horizon, existing examples).
2. Acknowledge uncertainty around the social cost of carbon figure and its political contestation across jurisdictions.
3. Briefly distinguish technology-specific differences in learning rates, capital structure, and MRV complexity.
4. Note scope conditions: what does and does not generalize beyond EU/US OECD contexts.

**Strengths to preserve:**
- The counterintuitive insight about reserve prices equaling the social cost of carbon is the paper's most distinctive and valuable contribution. It should be highlighted more prominently.
- The clear three-stage structure is an excellent organizing framework for what is otherwise a diffuse policy conversation.
- The comparison between future auctions and early offtake agreements is a useful and concrete framing of a real policy decision.
